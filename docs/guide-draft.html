How to use Moonlight

How to read what Moonlight finds.

Moonlight does one thing. You give it a disease, and it shows which medicines act on the proteins linked to that disease. This page explains each part of what you see, and what it does not mean.

Moonlight follows this path through open research data and shows you every step. It finds connections worth reading about. Scientists and doctors decide what they mean.

Three steps

A search takes a few seconds.

  1. Type a disease

    Use the name a doctor would write. If Moonlight is not sure, it asks “Did you mean?” and waits for you.

  2. Pick the one you mean

    Many diseases have several types. Moonlight lists them and never chooses for you.

  3. Open a tab

    The medicines are sorted into three tabs. Tap one to see its list.

The three tabs

Every medicine Moonlight shows is approved for at least one disease. The tabs tell you how it relates to the disease you searched.

Doxapram is a medicine. Doctors already use it for something else. It comes in more than one form. Doxapram grabs a protein called KCNK3. A protein is a tiny machine in your body. Doxapram turns KCNK3 down, like a volume knob.

A
The medicine and its forms
A medicine and its tablet forms are shown as one item. The number beside the name is its place in the list.
B
Reports
How many clinical reports the database holds for this medicine. More reports means more has been written about it. It does not mean a better clue.
C
The path
Medicine, then protein, then disease. If any step is missing, Moonlight does not show the card.
D
Up, down or unknown
What the medicine does to the protein, like a volume knob. On this same list, another medicine turns KCNK3 up. Both are clues, because the data does not say which way helps. “Unknown” means the database uses a word Moonlight does not recognise, so it does not guess.
E
Points out of 100
How strongly research links this protein to the disease. The points belong to the protein. They are not a grade for the medicine.
F
The explanation
The facts on the card, rewritten in plain words. Nothing is added that is not on the card.
G
The catch
The same on every card. The data does not say whether the disease needs the protein turned up or down, so a clue can point the wrong way.
H
Source record
A link to the public record behind the card, so you can check it yourself.

What Moonlight cannot tell you

Knowing these makes the rest more useful.

  • Whether a clue would help or harm. That takes trials in people.
  • Which direction the disease needs. A medicine that turns a protein the wrong way could make things worse.
  • Anything beyond the 25 most-linked proteins. Moonlight stops there for each disease.
  • Treatments that do not act on a protein. A moisturiser, a diet or surgery will never appear.
  • What is approved where you live. The database records approval in at least one country.
  • That the record is complete or current. Public databases have gaps and mistakes, and they change.

Good ways to use it

Moonlight is a place to start reading.

Use it to

  • learn which proteins are linked to a disease
  • find the medicines already approved or in trials
  • open the source record and read further
  • write down questions to take to your doctor

Do not use it to

  • start, stop or change any medicine
  • judge the medicine you take by its points
  • decide that a clue will work
  • look for where to get a medicine

Where the facts come from

Every fact has a public source.

The facts are from the Open Targets Platform. It is a free, open research database that brings together public sources such as ChEMBL for medicines, ClinicalTrials.gov for trials, Europe PMC for published research, and others. Moonlight shows which release of the data it is using.

The order is decided by plain rules. The ranking rule is printed above the list of new clues. No AI ranks or chooses anything.

The plain-language explanation is written by Gemini. It is given only the facts on the card, and its answer is checked against them. If Gemini is busy, Moonlight writes the explanation from its own template.
